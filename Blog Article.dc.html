Reading the Signals

A quick guide to what your companion is telling you

28 August 2026·6 min read·Mr Levi's Legacy

Our companions are always communicating — with their bodies, their posture, the smallest shifts in how they move. Here are the signals worth knowing. Keep it somewhere handy, and remember the golden rule below: read the whole animal, in the whole moment.

Dogs

Relaxed & happy
  • Loose, wiggly body; a sweeping wag that moves the whole rear end
  • Soft eyes and a relaxed, open mouth
  • Ears in a natural position, weight evenly balanced
  • A play bow, or an easy, waggy approach
Worried, or asking for space
  • Lip-licking or yawning when not hungry or sleepy
  • Turning the head or eyes away; leaning subtly away
  • A sudden sneeze, a full-body “shake-off,” or freezing in place
  • A raised front paw, a tucked tail, or “whale eye” (whites showing)
  • A high, stiff, fast wag — or a low, quick wag with a lowered body

Remember: a wag means the dog is aroused, not always happy. Read it with the whole body.

Cats

Content & trusting
  • A slow blink — a “kitten kiss.” Try slowly blinking back
  • A loose, fluid body and half-closed eyes
  • A tail held high with a gentle curl at the tip
  • Kneading, or gentle purring with a relaxed posture
Stressed or irritated
  • Flattened ears and dilated pupils
  • A tucked tail, or a flicking/swishing tail (irritation)
  • Puffed fur, a crouched body, or retreating to hide
  • Purring can also be self-soothing when anxious or in pain — read the context

Note: a cat's swishing tail is nearly the reverse of a dog's happy wag.

Birds

Relaxed & content
  • Soft, slightly fluffed feathers lying smooth — not slicked down
  • Gentle beak-grinding — a contented “sigh” before rest
  • Resting on one foot; a slow blink with soft, rounded eyes
  • Head lowered for a scratch, gentle head-bobbing, soft chatter
Tense, or asking for space
  • Feathers pressed tight to a slim body, or a crest flattened back
  • Rapid “eye-pinning” with a rigid body, fanned tail or open beak
  • Slicked neck feathers and sidling away — a clear “not now”
  • Hissing or growling; crouching or hiding

Note: eye-pinning can mean delight (a treat) or a warning — read it with the whole body. Prolonged fluffing with closed eyes and low energy can signal illness; see an avian vet.

Rabbits

Relaxed & happy
  • A flop onto their side, or loafing with legs tucked under
  • Sprawled out and stretched — deeply at ease
  • Ears in a soft, neutral position; a slow, steady nose-twitch
  • A “binky” (a joyful mid-air twist) or happy zoomies; nudging for attention
Frightened, or asking for space
  • Body flattened low, ears pinned back, eyes wide
  • A hind-foot thump — alarm, or “I'm not happy”
  • Freezing, hiding, or bolting for cover
  • Boxing (up on hind legs), lunging or grunting — give space

Note: with lop-eared rabbits, ear signals are subtler — read posture, eyes and nose instead. A hunched posture with loud teeth-grinding suggests pain; see a vet.

Guinea pigs

Happy & settled
  • “Popcorning” — sudden joyful jumps, often on fresh hay
  • A loose, relaxed body; stretching out or lying sprawled flat
  • Soft, low purring with a relaxed body; gentle “chutting” while exploring
  • “Wheeking” in happy anticipation; coming to you willingly
Stressed, or asking for space
  • Freezing — sudden stillness while they check for danger
  • Hiding more than usual, or running away; wide eyes, tense body
  • Teeth-chattering — a clear “please back off”
  • A high-pitched, tense purr (the opposite of the low, relaxed one)

Note: a low, loose purr is contentment; a high, tense purr is discomfort — the body tells you which. Eating less, hunching or hiding away can signal pain; see a vet.

The golden rule

No single signal ever means just one thing. A yawn in a sunny bed is tiredness; a yawn in a tense moment may be stress. Read the whole animal, in the whole moment, and against their own normal — and you'll rarely be far wrong.

This quick guide covers the companions most of us share our homes with. Horses, reptiles and others speak a language of their own — look to their dedicated body-language guides for those.
